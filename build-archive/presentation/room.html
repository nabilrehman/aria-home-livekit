Inside the Room

What a "room" really is, what the server in the middle is actually doing, and why it matters that the AI is a guest rather than the building.

Three ideas, taken slowly. These are the ones you'll lean on hardest at the whiteboard, and they're the ones most people get subtly wrong. No code until the last chapter, and that part is two commands.

01

Stop thinking about calls

When you picture a phone call, you picture a line between two people. You dial, a wire connects you, you talk, you hang up and the wire goes away. The call is the connection. If you want a third person, you need something extra bolted on — a conference bridge, a "merge calls" button.

Now picture a meeting room in an office instead. The room is just there. People walk in. Whoever is inside can hear whoever else is inside. Somebody new arrives halfway through and they simply… arrive — nobody has to reconfigure anything, because the room was never a connection between two specific people in the first place.

That second picture is what LiveKit means by a room. It is the single most important mental shift in this whole subject, and everything else gets easier once it lands.

So a room is not a wire. It's more like a guest list that the server maintains, and everyone on that list can hear everyone else.

Which raises a question

If the room is a place rather than a wire, then something has to be hosting that place. Where is the room, physically? And what is it doing?

02

Why there's a computer in the middle

Your voice has to physically get to the other people somehow. The simplest arrangement is for your laptop to send it straight to each of theirs. With two people that's fine — one stream out, one stream in.

With four people it stops being fine, and the reason is one-sided. Your download speed is usually generous; your upload speed is much smaller. But sending your voice directly to three other people means uploading three separate copies of it, simultaneously, forever. Add a fifth person and it's four copies. Your uplink is the first thing to break, and it breaks on the person with the worst internet — which in a support context is exactly the customer you most need to hear.

So instead everyone sends one copy to a computer in the middle, and that computer does the copying. It has a fat connection and it does this for a living. You upload once, no matter how many people are listening.

Which raises a question

Fine — but what does that middle computer actually do with your voice when it arrives? There are two very different answers, and picking the wrong one makes AI impossible.

03

A patch bay, not a mixing desk

Imagine a recording studio with four singers, each with their own microphone. There are two ways the engineer can hand that audio onward.

The mixing desk. Blend all four microphones into one finished stereo track and give everyone that. Convenient — one thing to send, one thing to play. But the moment it's blended, it's blended forever. You cannot pull one singer back out. And blending takes work: unpack all four, combine them, repack the result, which costs time you can feel on a live call.

The patch bay. Keep every microphone on its own separate channel and just route whichever channels somebody asked for. Nothing is unpacked, nothing is combined, nothing is rebuilt — signals are simply passed along the right wires. It's faster, and crucially every voice stays its own thing.

The computer in the middle of a LiveKit room is a patch bay. Its actual name is a Selective Forwarding Unit — an SFU — which is a fancy way of saying "it selectively forwards things." That's genuinely all the term means: it takes in streams and passes them along to whoever subscribed, without ever opening them up.

Keep this one

An SFU doesn't listen to your audio, understand it, or change it. It's a router that keeps every voice on its own channel and forwards each one to whoever asked. That's the whole job.

Worth saying out loud in the interview

On a one-to-one support call — one customer, one AI — the SFU isn't really saving you any bandwidth, because there's no crowd to fan out to. Admitting that yourself is a good move.

What it's actually earning its place for is the rest of it: a doorway close to the customer wherever they are, somewhere for phone calls to arrive, somewhere to keep the session alive if a machine dies, and the guest-list model that the next chapter is about.

Which sets up the good part

There's now a room, and a patch bay keeping every voice separate and routable. Which means anything that can hold a channel can join. Including something that isn't a person.

04

Whose idea is a room, anyway?

Worth stopping here, because it's a question that separates people who've actually built this from people who've read about it. Is a "room" part of WebRTC?

No. WebRTC has never heard of a room.

WebRTC gives you precisely one thing: a connection between two endpoints. The interface is even called RTCPeerConnection, and the spec describes it as establishing a connection "between two peers." Two. That's the unit. There is no concept of a group, a session with a name, a list of who's present, or permissions about who may speak.

It's stranger than that, though. WebRTC also deliberately refuses to tell you how the two peers find each other in the first place. That step — exchanging "here's what codecs I speak, here's where to reach me" — is called signaling, and the standard leaves it out on purpose, so that people could layer it over SIP, or XMPP, or a WebSocket, or anything else they liked.

Which means WebRTC hands you a beautifully engineered way to move audio between two machines, and total silence on every single question you actually have: who else is here, what are they called, how do I let someone in, how do I know when they leave.

So everybody invented their own

Every product in this space built that missing layer and gave it a different name. They are all the same idea:

None of those words appear in any standard. They're product decisions.

What your browser actually believes

Here's the part that makes it click. Sit in a room with five other people and your browser does not hold five connections. It holds exactly one — to the SFU. As far as WebRTC is concerned, your laptop is in a strictly two-party call with a server, which is the only thing WebRTC knows how to do.

The room isn't on your machine at all. It exists in the server's memory: a roster saying these six connections belong together. When somebody joins, the server updates the roster and tells everyone. The room is a fact the server keeps and tells you about — you never hold it yourself.

Two answers this hands you

"Are we locked in if we build on this?" — The media layer is standard WebRTC, so browsers, phones and codecs are portable. The room model, the participant identities and the agent framework are LiveKit's, so that's what you'd rewrite if you moved. Honest, specific, and much better than either "no lock-in!" or a shrug.

"Why can't we just use WebRTC and skip the vendor?" — Because WebRTC gets you a two-party call and stops. Everything you'd actually build a product out of — sessions, identity, presence, permissions, a server that can fan out to more than one listener — is the part that isn't in the standard. That's the part you'd be writing.

And now the useful consequence

If a room is just a roster the server keeps, then joining is just being added to a list. Which means anything that can hold a connection can be on that list — including something that isn't a person.

05

The guest list doesn't know what a robot is

Here's the part almost everyone gets wrong on the whiteboard, including people who know the technology well.

When you imagine adding AI to a phone system, you probably picture something like this: the call happens, and off to the side there's a service the audio gets fed into. The AI is behind the system. It's infrastructure. It's the switchboard.

That's not what happens. The AI walks into the room and sits down. It appears on the guest list. It publishes its voice on its own channel, exactly like you do, and it subscribes to yours, exactly like you would.

If you pull up the room's guest list mid-call, this is roughly what you see:

WhoTheir IDKindPublishing
the customerPA_7fK2nQ…STANDARDone audio channel
the AIPA_9mR4wZ…AGENTone audio channel

Same columns. Same shape. Same kind of entry. There is a field that says one of them is an agent — but it's a label, the way a visitor badge is a label. It doesn't make it a different kind of thing. Take that column away and you genuinely could not tell which row is the robot.

Concretely, the AI is a small program you run — on your laptop, in your cloud, wherever. It dials out to LiveKit and says "I'd like to join this room." Nothing is exposed, no ports are opened, no traffic is sent to you. It's a guest arriving, not a venue being booked.

And here's the payoff

This sounds like a philosophical distinction. It isn't. It quietly deletes about four features you'd otherwise have to build.

06

Four features you don't have to build

Ask each of these questions of the "AI is the building" model, and each one is a project. Ask them of the guest-list model, and each one has the same one-word answer.

The questionIf the AI is the buildingIf the AI is a guest
How does a human take over? Build a transfer system. Move the call somewhere. Carry the context across. Hope nothing drops. They join the room.
How do we record for compliance? Tap the audio somewhere, build storage, handle both sides separately. A recorder joins the room.
How does a supervisor listen in? Build a monitoring path that doesn't disturb the call. They join the room and stay quiet.
How do we add live translation? Insert something into the pipeline and rewire it. A translator joins the room.

That right-hand column is not a simplification for the sake of a nice table. It's genuinely how it works. Four things that look like four separate features turn out to be one feature you already have.

And it's why the handoff on your architecture diagram is drawn the way it is. The specialist doesn't receive a transferred call. They walk into a conversation that is already happening, with the history intact and the AI still sitting there — which is precisely why the customer never has to start their story again.

Say it out loud

"The thing I'd want you to notice here is that the AI isn't a service the audio gets piped into — it's a participant that joins the room, like a person would. Which sounds like a technicality until you ask what happens when a human needs to take over. In this model there's no transfer system to build. They just join the same conversation, and everything that's already happened is still there."

One thing left

You've been asked to take all of this on trust. You shouldn't have to — it takes about two minutes to watch it happen.

07

Watch the guest list

Everything above is already running on your machine. Two commands, two terminal windows side by side, and you can watch the room come into existence and the AI walk into it.

In the left window, start the guest-list watcher. It asks LiveKit once a second who's in which room, and prints anything that changed:

cd ~/Downloads/livekit
.venv/bin/python observe/watch_room.py

It'll sit there saying there are no rooms yet. That's correct — nobody has needed one.

In the right window, start the AI and open the link it prints:

cd ~/Downloads/livekit/anycompany-agent
lk agent dev

Click Start a session in the browser, then look back at the left window. You'll see something close to this:

00:41:12.204  ▣  room opened  playground-7Kd2
00:41:12.351  →  identity-9f4a joined playground-7Kd2  (browser user)
00:41:12.362  ·  now in room: identity-9f4a
00:41:13.118  →  anycompany-agent joined playground-7Kd2  (AI agent)
00:41:13.121  ·  now in room: anycompany-agent, identity-9f4a
00:41:13.489  ♪  anycompany-agent published a track

Read those six lines slowly, because all four chapters above are in them.

The room didn't exist until it was needed — line one is it being created, on demand, because somebody showed up. Nobody provisioned it.

You joined, then the AI joined — and they are the same kind of line. Same verb, same shape, same guest list. The only difference is the note in brackets, which the watcher works out by looking at the name.

The AI published a track — it put its voice on a channel, exactly as your browser did with your microphone. Now talk to it, and stop talking, and watch the room stay exactly as it is while the conversation happens. The membership is stable; only the audio moves.

Then close the browser tab and watch the last two lines arrive: everyone leaves, and the room closes itself. It was a session, not a server.

One more thing to try

With the call still live, open the same browser link in a second window. Watch a third name appear on the guest list.

That's the human handoff from your architecture diagram. Not a simulation of it — the actual mechanism, which is that a third participant arrived in a conversation already in progress. There was never anything else to build.

When you want to go deeper

There's a second tool sitting in observe/protocol_probe.py that does something different: instead of asking "who's here now?" once a second, it joins the room itself as an invisible guest and prints every announcement the server pushes out — somebody joined, a channel opened, this person is speaking now.

It's the same story at a lower level, and it's worth running once you're comfortable with the guest list, because it shows you that these announcements are actively broadcast to everyone in the room rather than being something you have to go and look up. But start with the guest list. It's the same lesson with less noise.

—

The two sentences

If the rest fades, these two are the ones that carry weight at a whiteboard, and they're worth being able to say without thinking:

  1. A room is a place, not a connection. Things join it. That's why adding a fourth participant halfway through a call requires nothing.
  2. The AI is a guest in that room, not the building. Which is why putting a human into the conversation later needs no new machinery at all.

Everything else in the architecture — the phone bridge, the model pipeline, the escalation path — hangs off those two.
