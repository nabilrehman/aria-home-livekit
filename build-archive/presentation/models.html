Voice Agents · Field Guide

The Model Decision

Which speech-to-text, language, and speech models to run — and how to reason about it out loud with a customer.

For: a pre-sales conversation about a voice agent Grounded in: docs.livekit.io/agents/models

Every voice agent has to do three things: hear you, think, and answer. The interesting part of a sales conversation is almost never whether to do those things — it's which model does each one, and why. This page is built to take you from "I know roughly what an LLM is" to being the calmest person in the room when a customer asks "so which models should we use?" We'll open the hood on how each stage actually works, learn the vocabulary you'll hear thrown around, and finish with a decision framework and a set of discovery questions you can run live.

The one idea to hold onto

There is no single "best" set of models, and confidently claiming there is will cost you credibility. Model choice is a set of tradeoffs against a specific customer's constraints — latency, languages, accuracy, cost, compliance, and how much control they need. Your job in the room isn't to name a winner. It's to ask the right questions and reason through the tradeoffs honestly, including when to tell them not to over-optimize.

Chapter 01

The two shapes a voice agent can take

Before any specific model, there's a fork in the road that colors every other decision, so we start here. A voice agent can be built in one of two shapes, and LiveKit supports both.

The first shape is the cascaded pipeline — also called sequential or STT-LLM-TTS. You chain together three specialized models. Audio comes in and a speech-to-text (STT) model transcribes it to text; a large language model (LLM) reads that text and writes a reply; a text-to-speech (TTS) model turns the reply back into audio. Three models, three clean handoffs, each one swappable without touching the others.

The second shape is a realtime model, sometimes called speech-to-speech or S2S. Here a single model takes audio in and produces audio out, with no transcription step on the way in and no separate synthesis step on the way out. It hears you and speaks back, directly.

The sibling pages in this series already walk through the pipeline mechanics and the latency budget in detail, so we won't re-derive them — the short version is that a natural conversation needs the agent to start responding in under about a second, and each model in the chain spends part of that budget. What this page cares about is the choice: which shape, and then which model in each slot. The figure below is the mental model to carry into every other chapter.

Chapter 02

Speech-to-text: turning sound into words

Start with the hardest-working, most underappreciated model in the chain. If STT mishears the customer, nothing downstream can recover — the LLM answers a question that was never asked. So a good STT choice quietly determines whether the whole agent feels competent.

What is STT actually doing? Sound arrives as a stream of numbers — a microphone sampling air pressure thousands of times a second. The sample rate is how many of those measurements happen per second; phone calls famously run at 8 kHz, a deliberately narrowband signal that throws away the high frequencies that distinguish "f" from "s." That's why telephone audio is harder to transcribe than a laptop mic, and it's a real thing to flag when a customer says "it's a call-center use case." The STT model's job is to map that raw waveform onto the sequence of phonemes — the atomic speech sounds — and then onto the most likely words, using a learned sense of how the language actually goes together ("reset my pin," not "reset my pen").

Streaming is the whole game for voice

Here's the distinction that matters most for voice, and it's worth being crisp about. A batch STT model waits for the whole audio clip, then returns a perfect transcript — great for transcribing a podcast, useless for conversation. A streaming STT model emits words as it hears them, revising as it goes. Those early, still-changing guesses are called interim results (or partials); the finalized text is the final. Streaming is what lets the agent begin thinking before you've finished your sentence, and it's what feeds live captions. For a voice agent, "does it stream?" is a gating question, not a nice-to-have.

Two more terms you'll hear. WER, word error rate, is the standard accuracy score — the percentage of words inserted, deleted, or substituted versus a human reference. Lower is better; single-digit WER on clean audio is strong, but WER climbs fast with accents, jargon, background noise, and narrowband phone audio, which is exactly why you test on the customer's real audio rather than trusting a headline number. Diarization is labeling who spoke — separating the agent, the caller, and a third party on the line — which matters for transcripts and analytics but less for the live loop.

Endpointing: knowing when they're done

An STT model also has to guess when you've stopped talking so the agent can reply. That's endpointing, and it's deceptively hard: pause too eagerly and you interrupt someone mid-thought; wait too long and the agent feels sluggish. Some newer STT models fold sophisticated end-of-turn detection right into the transcription step — we'll come back to this as its own model choice in Chapter 6, because LiveKit treats turn detection as a first-class decision.

Say it like this

For a phone-based agent, your STT choice is doing the heaviest lifting, because 8-kilohertz call audio is genuinely harder to transcribe than a laptop mic. I'd want to benchmark two or three providers on your actual call recordings — including the accents and the account-specific jargon — before we commit, rather than trust a leaderboard.

Chapter 03

The LLM: why voice is not chat

The LLM is the brain — it reads the transcript, decides what to do, and writes the reply. It's tempting to reach for the biggest, smartest model you know from chat. For voice, that instinct is often wrong, and understanding why is one of the most valuable things you can carry into a sales conversation.

Time-to-first-token beats raw brilliance

In a chat window, you'll happily wait three seconds for a brilliant answer. In a spoken conversation, three seconds of silence is unbearable — the customer thinks the line dropped. What matters is TTFT, time-to-first-token: how long until the model produces its first word, not its last. (A token is the chunk a model reads and writes in — roughly a short word or word-piece.) Because the pipeline streams, the TTS can start speaking the moment the first few tokens arrive, so a model that starts fast feels responsive even if it isn't the smartest model on the market. The related number is tokens-per-second, the streaming speed once it's going — it needs to keep ahead of the pace of natural speech, but past that threshold, more raw speed buys you little.

The counter-intuitive rule

For most voice agents, a fast mid-sized model beats a slow frontier model. The frontier model may reason better on paper, but the latency it adds makes the conversation worse, and a customer-service script rarely needs PhD-level reasoning. LiveKit's own default reflects this: they recommend Gemma 4 31B, a latency-optimized open-weight model served on their own infrastructure, precisely because it's tuned for the voice loop rather than for benchmark glory.

Instruction-following and tool calls

A customer-service agent rarely just chats — it has to do things: look up an order, check a balance, book an appointment. It does this through tool calls (function calls), where the model, instead of talking, emits a structured request like lookup_order(id) that your code executes. So the quality that matters isn't eloquence, it's reliable instruction-following: does the model call the right tool, with the right arguments, at the right moment, and stay on script? A smaller model that follows instructions crisply will outperform a bigger one that improvises. This is also, per the docs, an area where the cascaded pipeline's text-based tool calling is more mature and predictable than the audio-native alternative — a real point in the cascade's favor for transactional agents.

One more term: the context window is how much text the model can consider at once — the system prompt, the tools, and the running conversation all live there. Long calls, long knowledge-base snippets, and detailed instructions all consume it. It rarely decides the model choice for a short support call, but it's the thing to watch if the agent carries a large policy document or a very long conversation history.

Providers in LiveKit Inference · LLM

The catalog spans OpenAI (GPT family, including small "mini"/"nano" tiers built for speed), Google (Gemini Flash line, plus LiveKit's own Gemma default), DeepSeek, Moonshot Kimi, xAI Grok, and open-weight options like GPT-OSS. Note the pattern in the docs: models get retired on weeks of notice and the tables are full of "deprecated, use X instead." That's your cue to sell the gateway, not a model — an agent pointed at LiveKit Inference rides those swaps with a one-line change instead of a migration.

Chapter 04

Text-to-speech: what makes a voice sound human

TTS is where the agent gets a personality, and it's the stage customers react to most viscerally in a demo — a robotic voice sinks the whole experience no matter how smart the LLM is. So it's worth understanding what separates a natural voice from a stilted one.

Prosody is the difference

Reading words aloud correctly is the easy part; modern TTS nailed that years ago. The hard part is prosody — the melody, rhythm, stress, and intonation of speech. It's the rise at the end of a question, the emphasis that lands on the right word, the micro-pause before bad news. Older TTS stitched together recorded fragments and sounded flat because it had no model of prosody. Today's neural TTS predicts prosody from context, which is why the best voices can sound genuinely warm — and why some can now inject emotion, pacing, and even non-verbal sounds on cue (LiveKit exposes this as an "expressive mode" that lets the LLM mark up the delivery). For a consumer brand, prosody quality is often the single most scrutinized thing in the whole stack.

First-byte latency, again

Just like the LLM, TTS lives or dies on how fast it starts. The metric is TTFB, time-to-first-byte: how quickly the first chunk of audio comes back so the agent can begin speaking. A streaming TTS emits audio in chunks as the text arrives from the LLM, so the two stages overlap and the customer hears a reply almost immediately. A model with gorgeous output but slow first-byte will still feel laggy in conversation. Naturalness and speed are a genuine tradeoff here, and the right balance depends entirely on the use case — which is a decision, not a default.

Providers in LiveKit Inference · TTS

Cartesia (Sonic — low-latency, many languages), Deepgram (Aura), Fish Audio, Inworld, Rime, xAI, and ElevenLabs (known for expressive, characterful voices). Two capabilities worth knowing about because customers ask for them by name: custom voices — clone a brand voice from a short sample and reuse it across providers — and the expressive mode above. As always, providers and models churn; anchor on capability (latency, language coverage, voice cloning, expressiveness) rather than a model string.

Say it like this

For your brand, I'd treat the voice as a product decision, not a technical one — we should shortlist a couple of providers and let your marketing team actually listen. And if you want the agent to sound like a specific person, we can clone a voice from a short sample. The thing I'd watch is first-byte latency: the most beautiful voice still feels broken if it's slow to start talking.

Chapter 05

The other shape: realtime, speech-to-speech

Now open the dashed box. A realtime model collapses all three stages into one: audio in, audio out, no text in the middle. This isn't just a smaller diagram — it changes what the agent can do, for better and for worse, and knowing the mechanics lets you make the call with confidence instead of hype.

The upside is real and comes from the same fact: nothing gets flattened into text. Because the model hears the audio, it picks up prosody, tone, hesitation, sarcasm — verbal cues that vanish the instant speech becomes a transcript. And because it generates audio directly, its output can carry genuine emotion and emphasis rather than relying on a separate TTS to guess. Collapsing three hand-offs into one also removes inter-stage latency, so a realtime model has the lowest baseline delay of any option. When the experience needs to feel effortless and human — a companion, a coach, a natural-sounding front door — this is compelling.

The costs are just as real, and honesty here is what makes you trustworthy. With no text in the middle, you lose the transcript — the docs are explicit that realtime models don't emit interim transcripts, and user transcriptions can lag and even arrive after the agent has replied. You lose scripted speech: there's no reliable way to make the model say an exact sentence (no dependable say()), so a mandated legal disclosure or a fixed confirmation isn't guaranteed verbatim. Tool calling is less mature than the text path. You're locked to a single provider for the whole audio path. And auditing is harder — there's no clean text trail at every stage for QA or compliance. There's a middle option, the half-cascade: use a realtime model for understanding but hand its text reply to a separate TTS you control, buying back scripted output and voice control while keeping prosody-aware listening.

The honest default

Per LiveKit's own guidance: for most production agents, the cascaded pipeline is the right default. It's the easiest to debug, audit, and control, and its tool calling is the most reliable — which is exactly what a transactional customer-service agent needs. Reach for realtime when latency or emotional expressiveness genuinely outweigh control, and reach for half-cascade when you want realtime's ears but the cascade's mouth. Recommending realtime for a compliance-heavy support line, just because it's newer and faster, is the kind of mistake that surfaces in production.

Realtime providers in the docs

OpenAI Realtime API, Google Gemini Live, Amazon Nova Sonic, xAI Grok Voice, plus Phonic, Ultravox, and NVIDIA PersonaPlex. It's an early, fast-moving space — LiveKit adds providers as they emerge — so frame it as "a capability that's maturing," not a settled default.

Chapter 06

Turn detection is a model choice too

Here's a decision people forget is a decision: how does the agent know you've finished talking? Get it wrong and every other model's quality is wasted, because the agent either talks over the customer or leaves dead air. LiveKit treats this as its own model choice, and you should too.

The floor is VAD, voice activity detection — a lightweight model (LiveKit uses Silero) that just tells speech from silence. VAD alone is fast and cheap but naive: it hears a pause and assumes you're done. The classic failure is a caller who says "I'd like to book a flight… from Dublin… this Friday" — the natural pauses fool VAD into barging in before they've finished. The fix is a semantic turn detector: LiveKit's model reads both what was said and how it was said (intonation, rhythm) to predict whether the thought is actually complete, and it waits through those mid-sentence pauses. There's also barge-in — the mirror-image skill of noticing when the user interrupts the agent and gracefully yielding, so the customer never has to talk over a monologue.

The practical guidance from the docs: the semantic turn detector is the recommended default for cascaded pipelines; drop to VAD-only when you need absolute minimum latency or a language the detector doesn't cover; and when you use a realtime model, lean on its built-in turn handling, since the separate detector needs interim transcripts a realtime model doesn't provide. It's a small decision that punches far above its weight in how the conversation feels.

Chapter 07

LiveKit Inference: the gateway you're actually selling

Everything so far has been provider-neutral on purpose. Now the centerpiece, and the thing that ties the room together: LiveKit doesn't just support these models, it offers a unified gateway to them called LiveKit Inference. Understanding why it exists is most of the pre-sales value of this whole page.

The problem it solves is real. Without a gateway, a serious voice agent means separate accounts, API keys, rate limits, and invoices for Deepgram and OpenAI and Cartesia — three vendor relationships to manage before you've shipped anything. And every hop your audio takes to a far-away model API adds milliseconds you can't spare in a sub-second budget. LiveKit Inference answers both: it's one API, one bill, one set of rate limits for models across OpenAI, Google, AssemblyAI, Deepgram, Cartesia, ElevenLabs, and more, and it's co-located with the media path — the models run on the same infrastructure that's already carrying the call, so the audio doesn't detour across the internet to reach them.

The co-location argument — your strongest latency point

In a voice agent, latency is cumulative: STT, LLM, TTS, and every network hop between them all eat the same one-second budget. If each model lives at a different vendor's data center, the audio ping-pongs across the internet three times per turn. By running the models next to the media, LiveKit removes those detours. That's the crisp version of "why not just call the model APIs myself?" — you can, but you'll pay for it in the exact currency voice can least afford.

Zero data retention, by default

This one lands hard in regulated conversations, so keep it ready. LiveKit Inference is zero data retention (ZDR) by default: prompts, audio, and model outputs pass through only to generate the response — neither LiveKit nor the underlying providers log, store, or train on the data. It applies to every LLM, STT, and TTS model, on every plan, with no configuration. For a bank or a healthcare provider, "your customers' voice data isn't being retained or used to train anyone's model" is often the sentence that unblocks the deal.

The pricing shape

Bill it accurately and you'll never get caught out. Inference is usage-based, priced per unit of consumption, and included in LiveKit Cloud. The free Build plan includes a monthly allowance expressed as credits measured in USD that you can spend across any mix of models; on the free plan that allowance is a hard cap (requests fail rather than incur overage), it resets on the first of each month, and it doesn't roll over. Separately, every plan includes a generous pool of free inference requests per month for local development and testing (the docs cite 40,000), so building and iterating doesn't burn production budget. Discounted rates come on the Scale plan and custom rates on Enterprise. The exact dollar figures move, so quote the shape — usage-based, free credits to start, discounts as you scale — and point to the live pricing page for numbers.

Say it like this

Instead of you signing three separate model vendors and stitching their invoices together, LiveKit Inference gives you one API and one bill across all of them — and because the models run right next to the call media, you're not paying a latency tax to reach them. It's zero-data-retention by default, so nobody's storing or training on your customers' voices, and there are free credits to prototype before you commit to anything.

Chapter 08

Beyond the gateway: plugins, self-host, residency

Inference is the easy path, not a walled garden — and saying so out loud reassures customers who fear lock-in. Beyond it, LiveKit's plugin ecosystem lets you bring any supported provider with your own account and key, mixing freely: an Inference STT with a plugin LLM, or whatever the requirement dictates. Because so many providers speak the OpenAI API format, one plugin covers a long tail of them just by pointing at a different base URL. And the whole framework is open source, so a provider that isn't covered can be added.

Two reasons a customer reaches past the gateway, and you should welcome both. First, control: some teams want to own the vendor relationship, negotiate their own rates, or use a model that isn't in Inference yet — plugins let them keep their existing contracts. Second, and more strategic in enterprise deals, data residency. A regulated customer may be contractually barred from letting audio leave a specific jurisdiction or their own cloud. That's a model-topology question as much as a model-quality one: the answer may be self-hosting components, a provider with a regional endpoint, or LiveKit's region-pinning to keep traffic inside a geography for GDPR-style requirements. The point to internalize is that "which model" and "where the model runs" are two different axes, and in regulated deals the second one can override every performance argument on the first.

Don't over-promise here

Data-residency and self-hosting specifics vary by provider, plan, and region, and features like region-pinning sit on higher tiers. If a customer raises a hard residency requirement, the honest move is "that's a real constraint and it shapes the architecture — let's map exactly where the audio can and can't go," not an on-the-spot guarantee. Getting this wrong in the room is worse than saying "let me confirm the specifics."

Chapter 09

The decision framework — and the questions that get you there

This is the payoff. Everything above collapses into a handful of questions whose answers point at an architecture. You're not memorizing model names; you're running a diagnostic. The tree below is how to think on your feet, and the discovery questions after it are how you fill in the branches live.

The discovery questions to ask before you recommend anything

Ask these before naming a single model. The answers pick the architecture for you, and asking them is what separates a consultant from a catalog.

  1. Is this regulated, and what happens to the audio?Banking, healthcare, or a data-residency clause changes everything — it can force self-hosting or region-pinning and take realtime off the table. This is the first question because it can override every other answer.
  2. Do you need a transcript, and what for?QA scoring, compliance review, evals, and analytics all need text — and that's a strong vote for the cascade. If they say "we don't care about transcripts," realtime opens up. Have the reasons ready: this is where "why would I want a transcript?" comes up.
  3. Are there lines that must be spoken exactly?Legal disclosures, fixed confirmations, mandated scripts — these need reliable scripted speech, which points to cascade or half-cascade, not pure realtime.
  4. What's the latency bar, and on what network?A snappy consumer app has a tighter budget than a support line where callers expect a beat. And "is this over the phone?" matters — narrowband audio is harder for STT and shapes the provider choice.
  5. Which languages, and which accents?Language coverage varies sharply by provider and narrows the shortlist fast. Always test on the customer's real accents and jargon, not a benchmark.
  6. How transactional is it?Heavy tool-calling — lookups, bookings, actions — favors the cascade's mature, predictable function calling. A mostly-conversational agent has more freedom.
  7. How price-sensitive, at what volume?Model cost compounds over millions of minutes. But resist premature optimization: get it working and natural first, then tune cost. Cheapest-model-first is a common way to ship something customers hate.
  8. Build-vs-buy: how much do they want to own?A team that wants one bill and no vendor sprawl is a clean fit for LiveKit Inference. A team that insists on owning model contracts wants plugins. Neither is wrong — match the recommendation to the appetite.
The most credible thing you can say

Pre-sales credibility comes from restraint, not enthusiasm. The strongest move in the room is often: "Start with the cascaded pipeline on LiveKit Inference — it's the honest default, it's auditable, and it's swappable. Let's benchmark two or three providers on your real audio, ship it, and only reach for realtime or a fancier model where the data says the experience actually needs it." Naming the boring, correct answer — and being willing to say "you don't need that yet" — is what makes a customer trust you with the parts that are hard.
