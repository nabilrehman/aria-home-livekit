Talking While You Wait

What happens to a conversation when your agent has to go and do something slow — and the two things LiveKit gives you to fix it.

Everything here has numbers behind it. They came from a refund flow running against a deliberately slow backend, tested by voice, on your own machine.

01

The moment the conversation stops

Your agent is talking to someone. They ask for a refund. To actually do it, the agent has to call three separate systems — check the order is eligible, ask the payment processor, write a record. Together they take about six seconds.

Written the obvious way, that code does exactly what you'd expect:

await check_eligibility(order)     # 1.6s
await payment_processor(order)     # 3.2s
await write_ledger(order)          # 1.4s
return {"reference": reference}

And here is the problem, which is not obvious at all until you hear it: while that runs, the agent cannot speak. It has no reply yet, because the tool hasn't returned. So the caller gets nothing. No "one moment", no hold music, no breathing. Silence.

On a screen, six seconds of a spinner is fine. On a phone call, six seconds of silence is a dropped call.

And it's worse than six seconds

Because the person on the other end doesn't sit quietly through it. They react. And their reaction makes it longer.

That is the thing to understand: silence isn't a passive cost. It makes the customer speak, speaking creates a turn, the turn adds delay, and the delay creates more silence. The failure compounds itself.

Keep this one

The tester lasted 6.8 seconds before saying "are you there?" — and that was someone who knew it was a test and knew the backend was slow. A real customer with a real problem cracks sooner.

Which is why

LiveKit gives you two separate ways to make noise while a tool is still running. They sound similar and they are not.

03

Two channels, two different jobs

The framework's answer is a pair of tools that both fill silence but travel completely different routes to the speaker.

The one that matters most

The first ctx.update() is what makes the tool non-blocking. Before it, the conversation is stuck waiting on your tool. After it, the agent has the floor back and can keep talking while your slow work carries on underneath.

A tool that never calls update() behaves like an ordinary blocking tool, no matter what else you do.

And here's where it went wrong

Both of these were used correctly on the first attempt, and one of them still did absolutely nothing. It took measuring to notice.

04

The trap in the word "delay"

Filler speech takes a delay. The natural reading is "wait this long into the tool, then say something." That reading is wrong, and it produces a feature that silently never runs.

delay means: how long the whole session must be continuously quiet before the filler is allowed to fire. Not quiet inside your tool — quiet on the call. And every time anyone speaks, including your own progress updates, that timer resets to zero.

Why this is worth remembering

This is the most useful kind of bug: the API was called correctly, the arguments were valid, nothing threw, and the feature was inert. The only way to catch it was to measure the silence and notice a line that should have appeared and didn't.

So the shape that works

One update to open, then a filler scope around each slow phase, sized to that phase.

05

What good looks like

# Opens the conversation back up. The FIRST update is what makes
# the tool non-blocking, so this line matters more than it looks.
await ctx.update("Starting the refund. This takes a few seconds.")

# Phase 1 — short. One filler is enough if they go quiet.
async with ctx.with_filler("Just checking that order, one moment.", delay=2):
    await check_eligibility(order)
await ctx.update("The order is eligible. Sending it to the processor.")

# Phase 2 — the long one. A callable rotates the lines so it
# never repeats itself, which is what makes a bot sound broken.
lines = ["Still with the payment processor, hang tight.",
         "Almost there, just waiting on their confirmation."]
async with ctx.with_filler(lambda step: lines[step],
                           delay=2, interval=4, max_steps=len(lines)):
    await payment_processor(order)
await ctx.update("The processor accepted it. Writing the record now.")

# Phase 3 — short.
async with ctx.with_filler("Nearly done.", delay=2):
    await write_ledger(order)

# Spoken automatically as a follow-up once the agent is idle.
# No extra update() needed here.
return {"reference": reference, "arrives_in": "three to five business days"}

Four things in there are deliberate. One update before anything slow, because that's what hands the conversation back. A scope per phase, so each gets its own quiet window. A short delay, tuned to the actual gaps rather than guessed. And a callable for the long phase, so repeated fillers say different things.

Say it out loud

"Anything slower than a second or two needs to talk while it works. There are two channels — one puts a status into the conversation so the model voices it naturally, the other plays a fixed line straight to the speaker during quiet gaps. The gotcha is that the filler delay is measured against session silence, not against your tool, so your own progress updates reset it. I had that wrong the first time and my filler never fired at all."

—

Four things to carry

  1. Silence on a phone call reads as a dropped call, and past a few seconds the customer starts talking — which makes it worse, not better.
  2. The first ctx.update() is the switch. It hands the conversation back and makes everything after it non-blocking.
  3. Updates are information, fillers are noise. One goes through the model, one goes around it.
  4. delay counts session silence, not tool time. Wrap each slow phase separately, or your filler will never fire and nothing will tell you.
