Wiring Up a Phone Number

What has to exist before a telephone can reach your agent — trunks, numbers, dispatch rules — plus the two things people confuse them with.

Read it with a phone in your hand. The last chapter is a live number and a menu of things to say to it.

01

Four things, and only two are yours

Someone dials a number and ends up talking to your agent. Between those two moments sit four things, and the useful surprise is how few of them you have to build.

So the first question in any telephony conversation

Is not "how do I configure this" — it's "whose number is it?" Everything else follows from that answer.

02

The trunk, and why you might not need one

The word comes from the old telephone exchanges. A local line ran to one house. A trunk line was the thick bundle of wires running between two exchanges, carrying many conversations at once. That's the whole image, and it survived into the internet era intact.

The modern version has no wires. A SIP trunk is a contract, not a cable. It's a configuration object that says: calls arriving from this address, for these phone numbers, proving themselves this way, are allowed in. That's it. Delete the object and the calls stop being accepted — nothing physical changes.

What's actually inside one

How a call proves it's allowed in

Two ways, and the choice is more consequential than it looks.

Credentials — a username and password, the way you'd expect. Clean, portable, works from anywhere. But support varies by carrier: Twilio's Elastic SIP Trunking doesn't support it on inbound at all, and authenticates by source IP instead.

IP allowlisting — "only accept calls from these addresses." No password to leak, but it breaks the moment your carrier changes infrastructure, and it's weaker than it sounds. Telnyx has a genuine trap here: if LiveKit doesn't send a username on the first attempt, Telnyx matches on source IP alone — and that IP might belong to a different customer of theirs. The fix is forcing a password challenge by sending the username in a header.

That Telnyx detail is worth remembering

Not because you'll configure Telnyx, but because it's the kind of thing a security reviewer asks about and almost nobody has an answer for. Knowing that a carrier can mis-attribute a call by IP is a genuinely senior observation.

Inbound and outbound are not mirror images

That "always optional" on the outbound side surprises people. If your configuration is stable you store it once and reference the ID. If every customer brings their own carrier — a multi-tenant platform — you pass the trunk config inline with each call and store nothing.

The mistake worth knowing

People create a trunk per call, usually to vary the caller ID. Trunks are long-lived cached objects, and creating one per call bypasses that cache and degrades reliability at scale.

One trunk per number, reused forever. Per-caller isolation comes from the dispatch rule, not the trunk. To vary caller ID, set the number on the call request instead.

The one-line version

A trunk is the agreement between you and a phone carrier about which calls are allowed and how many fit at once. It's configuration, not infrastructure — and if you rent your number from LiveKit, you never create one.

03

The dispatch rule is the routing table

This is the only inbound object that is never optional, and it's where all the interesting decisions live. It answers two questions: which room does this caller land in, and which agent gets summoned there.

The second half is a field called roomConfig.agents, carrying an agentName. That string has to match the name in your code exactly:

# in the dispatch rule
"roomConfig": { "agents": [{ "agentName": "anycompany-agent" }] }

# in your Python
@server.rtc_session(agent_name="anycompany-agent")

That's the entire link between "a phone rang" and "your process wakes up." Get it wrong and the call connects to a room where nobody is listening.

Three types, and the choice is architectural

Two traps

Empty trunk list matches everything. A rule with no trunk_ids applies to all trunks, so two such rules compete unpredictably. With rented numbers you disambiguate by assigning the rule to the number; with your own trunks you must scope it.

Individual rules put the caller's phone number in the room name. Room names land in logs and traces, and PII redaction does not strip them. Use hide_phone_number, or pre-create a room with an opaque ID and route to that.

The passthrough that makes one agent serve many brands

A rule can carry metadata and attributes through to the agent, and the two arrive at different times. Job metadata reaches your agent at dispatch, before the caller is even in the room — so you can pre-load a knowledge base or warm a connection. Participant attributes arrive with the caller.

That's how you run one deployment across billing, sales and support: one rule per number, each carrying its own queue and configuration, all pointing at the same agentName.

Before the demo, two things people confuse this with

One is a genuinely different product. The other is a word that means three different things.

04

Ingress is not this

Ingress also brings outside media into a room, which is why it gets confused with SIP. It is not a telephony feature and it will not answer a phone.

Where it legitimately appears near a contact centre: hold music or brand audio published from a file, a live all-hands or training feed being transcribed, a supervisor's camera joining a video escalation. For a voice agent, it's almost always irrelevant.

Say it like this

"Ingress and SIP are both media bridges, but SIP is bidirectional and carries call signalling — ringing, answering, DTMF, hangup. Ingress is a one-way publish. For a voice agent, SIP is the whole story; Ingress only enters the picture if you're bringing broadcast or file-based media into the same room."

05

"Region" means three different things

By default, calls route to whichever LiveKit region is closest to their origin, and that is the recommended setup. Pinning exists for one reason — regulation, not performance — and it costs you something real.

AxisWhat it controlsValues look like

Outbound originationa parameter, not an endpoint — where your outbound calls appear to come fromdestination_country: "de"
Agent deploymentwhere your worker container runsus-east · eu-central · ap-south

Three separate lists, and they don't share a vocabulary. The trap worth remembering: sa means South America for realtime and Saudi Arabia for SIP.

The architectural point is co-location. If your SIP terminates in Frankfurt and your agent runs in us-east, you have added a transatlantic hop to every single turn — which is the latency lever from the primer, thrown the wrong way.

The honest trade

Pinning turns off automatic cross-region failover. You are exchanging redundancy for compliance. If someone asks for it purely to reduce latency, push back — global routing already picks the nearest region, and pinning can only make it worse.

Each of these exercises something different. Say the order number slowly, digit by digit — the phone leg gives speech recognition a much poorer signal than a browser does, and you will hear the difference.

What's the status of order four four seven two one?

Proves the whole inbound path — number, dispatch rule, agent dispatch, room creation — in one sentence.

It should say the thermostat has shipped and give a delivery date. Nothing in the agent's code knows this is a phone call.

Where exactly is my package for order four four seven two one?

Proves filler speech covering an unpredictable backend.

The carrier call is deliberately slow. Listen for the holding lines escalating — "still waiting", then "they're being slow today" — and count how long you're comfortable before you'd normally say "hello?"

I'd like a refund on order four four seven two one.

Proves progress updates and filler working together across a multi-step operation.

Confirm the number when asked. Then listen for it narrating each phase in its own words rather than going quiet for six seconds.

What's the status of order nine nine nine nine nine?

Proves it refuses to invent an answer.

It should say the order doesn't exist and ask you to check. If it ever describes a status for 99999, that is a hallucination and the most important bug in the system.

It's four four seven… [pause] …two one.

Proves semantic turn detection.

Leave a real gap in the middle. A system using silence alone would cut you off after "seven" and look up a three-digit number.

I don't want to talk to a bot. Get me a person.

Proves escalation as an arrival rather than a transfer.

It should escalate immediately without insisting on troubleshooting first. An explicit request for a human must always work — refusing it is the fastest way to make someone hate a voice agent.

The comparison worth making

Ask the same question in a browser and on the phone, and listen to the transcripts diverge. The phone gives you roughly 8 kHz narrowband against Opus at 48 kHz, so recognition is measurably worse on the same words.

That's a real constraint to raise with a customer before they discover it, and a good reason to evaluate the phone path separately rather than assuming web results carry over.

—

Five things to carry

  1. The dispatch rule is the only inbound object you can't skip. Rent the number and the trunk disappears entirely.
  2. agentName is the whole linkage between "a phone rang" and "your process wakes up." It has to match your code exactly.
  3. Individual rules leak the caller's number into room names, which reach logs and traces and are not covered by PII redaction.
  4. Ingress is a one-way publish. No signalling, no dialling, no PSTN. It cannot answer a phone.
  5. Pin a region for regulation, never for latency — and remember you're giving up automatic failover to do it.
