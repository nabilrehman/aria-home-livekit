The brief

Mike will describe a company with a support problem and ask you to draw the system with him. The scenario on the reference sheet is AnyCompany Smart Home — connected devices, high contact volume, customers arriving from both the web and the phone.

Reconstructed from that sheet, the question sounds roughly like this:

The question

"AnyCompany Smart Home sells connected devices and gets a lot of support contacts. Design a production voice AI agent: customers reach it from the web and over the phone, it should feel like a real conversation — fast, interruptible — it needs to look up customer, device and order data, file tickets in their ticketing system, and hand off to a human specialist when it can't resolve. Let's draw it. Walk me through the components, the integrations, where the latency goes, and what happens when things break."

There is a real chance you get a different scenario. The document that example came from is titled Architecture Draw Example and runs to five pages, and the recruiter wrote that Mike would walk you through "a realistic scenario" rather than "the" scenario. So treat the smart-home version as one worked example of a shape you should be able to produce for retail returns, appointment booking, outbound collections, or patient intake just as easily.

Two things about the format matter more than any single fact you could memorize.

It is collaborative, not an examination. "Sketch out the architecture together" means Mike expects to be drawn with. Long silences while you think are the main failure mode, and so is producing the finished picture immediately — that reads as recital rather than reasoning. He is watching how you work through a problem in front of a customer, because that is the actual job.

It is a pre-sales interview wearing a systems-design costume. Most people who fail this round know the technology perfectly well. They fail because they solve before they ask what the constraints are, because they give a tour of components instead of telling a story, and because they have no honest answer when someone pushes on cost, scale, or why not just use Twilio. Technical correctness is the floor here, not the bar.

01

The architecture, whole

Here is the reference answer, redrawn. Before you study any individual box, look at the shape: three vertical bands, and three distinct kinds of line running between them. The bands are the people, the realtime platform, and your own cloud. The lines are realtime media, ordinary data calls, and the human handoff. Every section after this one explains a single part of this picture.

What each band is doing

On the left are the people, and there are two ways in. Someone on the website clicks a button to talk to support, and someone else picks up a phone and dials. What matters is that these two paths converge almost immediately, and everything downstream is identical regardless of which door the customer came through. That convergence is most of the reason this architecture is worth building.

In the middle is LiveKit Cloud, which does three jobs: it moves audio reliably over networks that are frequently terrible, it bridges into the phone network, and it can host the models. What you are buying here is transport and physical proximity — the models are close to the media, which turns out to matter more than almost anything else you can tune.

On the right is your own cloud, holding your logic, your data and your integrations. Notice how thin this band is: a worker process, a web application, and a database. That thinness is not an accident and it is worth pointing out, because it is the difference between shipping in weeks and shipping in quarters.

The detail worth being able to defend

The ticketing box is drawn dashed and the customer database is not. That is an argument rather than a decoration: reads against data you own stay direct, while writes into someone else's system cross a declared boundary. The MCP service is where schema drift, authentication, rate limits and retries get contained.

If Mike pushes back that MCP is overkill for one integration, agree with him — because he is right — and then name the threshold at which it starts paying for itself. Agreeing and then adding something is a much stronger move than defending the diagram.

02

WebRTC and the SFU

What you need here is not the specification. It is the ability to answer "why not just use a WebSocket?" and "what is the SFU actually doing on a two-party call?" without hedging.

Why WebRTC rather than a WebSocket

A WebSocket runs on TCP, and TCP guarantees that everything arrives, in order. That sounds like exactly what you want, and for live audio it is precisely wrong. When a packet goes missing, TCP stops and asks for it again, and everything behind it waits in line. You did not lose twenty milliseconds of speech — you inserted a couple of hundred milliseconds of delay into the call, and you never get that time back. Every subsequent loss adds more, so the conversation drifts steadily further behind real time exactly when the network is worst.

WebRTC runs on UDP instead, where a lost packet is simply gone and the decoder covers the hole. Opus carries forward error correction and does packet-loss concealment, so losing two percent of packets is inaudible rather than being a series of small stalls. On top of that, WebRTC brings a jitter buffer that smooths uneven arrival into steady playback, bandwidth estimation that lowers the bitrate before congestion turns into loss, and echo cancellation — which matters more than it sounds, because without it the agent hears itself and starts answering its own questions.

The honest summary is that TCP trades latency for completeness, and live voice needs the opposite trade. You can build voice on WebSockets, and plenty of products do. It works beautifully on an office network and falls apart on a phone walking through a parking garage.

Peer-to-peer, MCU, and SFU

If everyone connects directly to everyone, each participant has to upload a separate copy of their audio to every other participant. That is fine at two people and becomes quadratic quickly, and the uplink is the scarce resource. It also makes it impossible to record the call, bridge it to a phone, or insert an AI — because there is no server in the middle to attach any of that to.

So you put a server in the middle, and there are two ways to build one. An MCU decodes everybody's audio, mixes it into a single combined stream and re-encodes it, which is cheap for the participants and expensive for the server. The transcoding adds latency, and worse, it destroys the separation between speakers — and an AI needs one person's clean audio, not a blend. An SFU receives each stream and forwards it selectively to whoever subscribed, without decoding or re-encoding anything. It adds very little delay, and the streams stay separate.

The SFU is the right answer, and separation is the reason that matters most here.

Say this before Mike says it

A one-to-one support call is the easy case for an SFU — you are not buying it for fan-out, and pretending otherwise is the kind of oversell that loses a technical audience. What you are actually buying is the edge close to the user, the phone bridge, session migration when an instance dies, and the participant model that makes both the AI and the human specialist first-class members of the same conversation. Naming that limitation yourself is worth considerably more than defending it after he points it out.

ICE, STUN and TURN — the corporate firewall question

Most machines sit behind NAT and have no public address of their own. Your router has one and everything in the house shares it, which works fine when you start the conversation and fails completely when someone else wants to start one with you. Both ends of a call have this problem at the same time.

STUN is a small public server you ask "what do I look like from out there?", and it tells you the public address and port your traffic appears to come from. That is usually enough. TURN is for when the network refuses UDP entirely, which locked-down corporate networks routinely do: a relay receives your media and forwards it, typically over TCP on port 443 so it looks like ordinary web traffic. It costs latency and bandwidth, but the call connects. ICE is the procedure that gathers all of these candidate paths, tries them simultaneously, and keeps whichever works best.

Rooms, participants and tracks

Three nouns, and together they are the entire data model. A room is one session. A participant is anything in it — a browser, a phone caller, an AI agent, a human specialist, a recorder. A track is one stream of media a participant publishes, and participants subscribe to each other's tracks.

The word doing the work is anything. Because the system does not distinguish between kinds of participant, several problems that look separate collapse into one solved problem: how the AI hears the customer, how you transfer to a human, how you record for compliance, and how a supervisor listens in are all the same answer.

Say it out loud ~40 sec

"The reason this is WebRTC and not a WebSocket is that TCP guarantees delivery, and for live audio that's the wrong guarantee. Lose a packet and TCP stops everything to go get it again, so you don't lose twenty milliseconds of speech — you permanently insert a couple of hundred milliseconds of delay, and it accumulates for the rest of the call. UDP just drops it and the codec fills the gap, which your ear won't notice.

The SFU in the middle isn't really about fan-out on a one-to-one call — I'd be honest about that. What it buys you is an edge close to the user, somewhere to terminate the phone bridge, and the model where the AI joins as a participant rather than being a service you pipe audio to."

Drill

Why not just stream audio over a WebSocket to your server?

You can, and it works until the network is bad. TCP head-of-line blocking turns packet loss into accumulating delay you never recover from within the call. UDP plus Opus forward error correction degrades gracefully instead. You would also be rebuilding jitter buffering, bandwidth estimation and echo cancellation yourself, and you would still have no answer for phone calls.

What is the SFU doing for a 1:1 call — isn't it just overhead?

For pure media routing on a two-party call it is close to overhead, and I would say so rather than oversell it. What it buys is everything around the media: an edge close to the user in whatever region they are in, session migration when an instance dies, a place for the phone bridge to terminate, and the participant model that makes the agent and the human specialist first-class members of the same session.

A customer's agents are on a locked-down corporate network. Will audio connect?

Yes. ICE gathers candidate paths and falls back to a TURN relay over TCP on 443 when UDP is blocked, so it looks like normal HTTPS traffic. It adds latency and relay bandwidth cost, so I would want to know what fraction of their users are in that situation before I quoted a latency number.

Where does the agent's audio actually go?

The agent worker connects to the room as a participant, subscribes to the customer's audio track and publishes its own. So the media path runs from the customer to the edge SFU and on to the agent worker — not through your web app, which only ever mints tokens and serves the interface.

03

The one-second budget

Mike will ask where the latency goes. Know this section cold, because it is the one place where a specific number beats a confident adjective.

The target is under one second, measured from the moment the customer stops speaking to the moment they hear the agent begin. That number is not an engineering preference — it comes from how people actually talk. Across the languages studied in a well-known comparison, the gap between one speaker finishing and the next beginning clusters in the low hundreds of milliseconds. Past roughly a second, people assume the line has dropped, or they start repeating themselves, which makes the following turn worse still.

StageBudgetWhat is happening
Audio transport< 50 msWebRTC to the nearest edge and on to the agent. This is why edge proximity matters.
End-of-turn decision~ 300 msDeciding the customer is actually finished rather than pausing. Pure waiting, and the biggest single lever.
STT first partial100–200 msStreaming, so partial transcripts arrive while they are still speaking. Only the final flush is on the critical path.
LLM first token200–400 msModel size, prompt length and tool calls all move this. A tool round-trip can double it.
TTS first audio100–300 msStreaming synthesis starts on the first clause rather than the finished sentence.
Perceived total< 1 sLess than the sum, because the stages overlap.

The three levers, largest first

Co-location is the big one, and it is structural rather than a tuning knob. Every stage in that pipeline might live in a different data center, and you pay the round trip between them four or five times per turn — audio in, STT, LLM, TTS, audio out. Putting inference physically next to the media path removes all of those hops at once, and it is worth more than any amount of model selection. This is also the sentence that ties the product to the physics, so it is worth saying out loud in the interview.

End-of-turn tuning is the second. Three hundred milliseconds of the budget is the system waiting to be certain, and shortening that makes the agent feel snappier while making it interrupt people more often. The next section is entirely about that trade.

Model choice is third, and less interesting than people expect. A smaller, faster model on the conversational path, escalating to something larger only when a turn genuinely needs it. Streaming synthesis rather than batch. Both trade quality for milliseconds in ways the customer should get to decide.

Cascaded pipeline against speech-to-speech

STT → LLM → TTS default here

Three swappable components, so you can choose each vendor, fail over per component, and tune cost per stage independently.

You also get a transcript for free, which matters enormously in support: that transcript is simultaneously the QA record, the compliance artifact, the analytics source and the eval set.

Speech-to-speech realtime

One multimodal model consuming and producing audio directly. Faster, often under 500 ms, with noticeably better prosody, because it hears tone and hesitation that a transcript discards.

The cost is control: less ability to constrain behavior, harder to swap pieces, usually more expensive, and you work harder for a reliable transcript.

The pre-sales answer to "which should we use" is a question back rather than a recommendation: is this a regulated support line where you need the transcript and predictable behavior, or a consumer experience where naturalness wins? For AnyCompany's warranty and order support, cascaded is the right default, and you can say so with reasons rather than as a preference.

Say it out loud ~45 sec

"The target is under a second, from the end of their speech to the start of ours — and that's not an arbitrary engineering number, it's roughly where human conversational timing sits. Past a second people think the call dropped.

Transport is under fifty milliseconds. The big one is actually end-of-turn detection, about three hundred milliseconds of just being sure they've finished. Then two to four hundred to first token from the model, and one to three hundred to first audio out of TTS. It comes in under a second rather than being the sum, because speech recognition is running while they're still talking — that part's already paid for by the time it matters.

And the biggest lever isn't model choice, it's co-location. If your inference is in a different region from your media, you're paying that round trip four or five times every single turn."

Drill

The customer says 800 ms feels sluggish. What do you cut?

First I would measure where it is actually going rather than guess, with per-stage metrics on every turn. In practice the order is: check co-location, then tune the end-of-turn threshold down and watch the false-cutoff rate move, then swap to a faster model on the common path. I would also check whether tool calls are sitting on the critical path, because a synchronous CRM lookup can cost more than the entire rest of the budget.

A tool call needs 900 ms. Now what?

Cover it with speech. The agent says "let me pull that up" and the lookup runs underneath — filler is a legitimate technique rather than a hack, because it is exactly what a person does. Beyond that: prefetch on intent rather than waiting for the explicit request, cache the customer record at call start using the caller ID, and make slow integrations asynchronous so a ticket write never blocks the conversation.

Why does LiveKit host inference at all — isn't that outside their lane?

Because latency is dominated by distance, and this is the one place they control both ends. If the media terminates at their edge and the model runs in that same facility, several cross-region round trips per turn disappear. It is not a play to replace model vendors; it is the same co-location argument that makes the rest of the product work.

04

Turn-taking, the hard part

Deciding when a customer has finished speaking is what makes a voice agent feel human or infuriating. It gets far less attention than latency, and it is where the genuinely interesting engineering lives.

There are two mechanisms, and they are doing different jobs. VAD — voice activity detection — is a small, fast, cheap model that runs continuously on the raw audio and answers exactly one question: is there speech right now? It understands nothing about meaning. Its jobs are noticing that the customer has started talking, which is how you get barge-in, and noticing silence.

Semantic end-of-turn detection answers a different question entirely: given what they have said so far, does it sound finished? LiveKit's is an open-weights transformer that reads the running transcript and predicts end of utterance in roughly fifty milliseconds. It has no ears at all; it works purely on text.

You need both, because they fail in opposite directions. VAD on its own cuts people off every time they pause to remember something. The semantic model on its own has no way to trigger barge-in and no cheap continuous signal to work from.

The trade-off curve, which is the thing to really understand

Turn detection is a dial with pain at both ends. Wait longer and you cut people off less often, but the agent feels slow. Wait less and it feels snappy and talks over people. There is no setting that removes the trade-off — a better model gives you a better curve, not an escape from it. LiveKit's own published framing is exactly this: at a three hundred millisecond latency budget, the v1 model sits at roughly a ten percent false-cutoff rate, described as a better frontier than the alternatives rather than a solved problem.

What makes this a strong interview answer is recognizing that the right setting is per use case rather than global. A customer reading out a sixteen-digit account number wants a patient agent. Someone answering yes or no wants a fast one. That is a configuration conversation to have with the customer, and noticing that is more sophisticated than any particular number.

Barge-in

When the customer starts talking while the agent is mid-sentence, three things have to happen quickly: stop the audio playback, discard the rest of the generated response, and correct the conversation history so the model knows only part of its reply was actually heard. That last step is the one naive implementations skip, and it produces agents that confidently refer back to things the customer never heard them say.

Say it out loud ~40 sec

"There are two models doing different jobs here. VAD is cheap and continuous — it just detects whether someone's speaking, and that's what gives you barge-in, so the customer can cut the agent off the way you would with a person.

But VAD on its own cuts people off, because a pause while you're remembering your order number looks exactly like being finished. So on top of it there's a small transformer reading the running transcript and asking 'does this sound complete?' — about fifty milliseconds. If the sentence ends on 'my order number is', it waits.

It's a dial, though, and there's no setting that makes the trade-off disappear. Shorter wait, snappier agent, more interruptions. Around a three-hundred-millisecond budget you're looking at roughly a ten percent false-cutoff rate. Where you set it really should depend on whether your customers are reading out account numbers or saying yes and no."

Drill

Our agent keeps interrupting customers. Fix it.

First I would confirm it is turn detection and not something else, because barge-in misfiring on background noise looks identical to the user. If it is endpointing, raise the threshold and measure what it costs in latency, and add the semantic model if they are running VAD-only. If it is noise, enable noise cancellation on the input. And if their customers routinely read out long numbers, that is a use-case-specific tuning conversation rather than a global default.

Why not just wait longer? Isn't 1.5 seconds fine?

It is fine right up until it is not. Long silences read as a dropped call, so people start repeating themselves, which makes the transcript worse and the turn longer still. You have also blown the sub-second budget entirely. The point of the semantic model is that it lets you wait a long time when the sentence sounds unfinished and a short time when it does not, instead of paying the worst case on every single turn.

What breaks if the customer is on a noisy factory floor?

VAD starts firing on machinery, which triggers spurious barge-in and cuts the agent off mid-sentence. Noise cancellation on the inbound track is the first fix. Beyond that, recognition accuracy drops, which degrades the semantic turn model too since it reads the transcript. So I would set expectations that this environment needs its own tuning and almost certainly its own eval set.

05

Agents, tools and the MCP boundary

This is the right-hand band of the diagram. Small surface area, but worth being precise about, because "where does our code run" is a question every customer asks within the first ten minutes.

The worker model

You run a long-lived process, called an agent server or worker. It registers with LiveKit and then waits. When a room needs an agent, LiveKit dispatches a job to a worker that has capacity, and that worker spawns an isolated subprocess which joins the room as a participant and runs your entrypoint function. By default, agents are dispatched to every new room in your project.

Four properties of this model are worth knowing, because between them they answer most reliability questions. Each job is process-isolated, so one call crashing does not take down the others on that worker. Dispatch is load-aware: workers report their capacity, and sessions are placed by effective load — accounting for user locality, CPU, memory and network — rather than by round-robin connection count. Sessions are inherently sticky, because a call lasts minutes and carries state, so it stays on the worker it started on; this is why agent workloads are not stateless web services, and it shapes every deployment decision downstream. And workers drain on deploy, meaning a worker being replaced stops accepting new jobs and lets existing calls finish rather than dropping them.

Sizing, and the number to have ready

A Python worker holds roughly five to twenty concurrent sessions per CPU core, varying a great deal with which plugins are in the pipeline. Agents are resource-heavy: this is tens of concurrent calls per machine, not hundreds.

Which means the t3.small on the reference diagram — two vCPUs and two gigabytes — is a demonstration box. It might carry ten or twenty concurrent calls with inference offloaded, and on that same sheet it is simultaneously running the Next.js application and the MCP service. Notice this out loud. Catching that the reference architecture is sized for a proof of concept, and then describing what production would actually look like, is one of the strongest single moves available to you in this interview.

Tools, and where the boundary goes

The agent's model is given a set of callable functions — look up a customer, check an order, create a ticket. In LiveKit Agents these are ordinary Python functions with a decorator, and the docstring is what teaches the model when to call them. That makes tool descriptions prompt engineering rather than documentation, which is a useful thing to point out.

MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is a standard way for a tool provider to describe what it offers so that any compatible agent can discover and call it. The agents SDK includes a toolset that wraps an MCP server, connects to it, fetches its tools and exposes them to the model.

Direct function tool

Your code, your database, running in-process. Fewer moving parts, the lowest latency, and versioned together with the agent.

Right for reads against data you own — on this diagram, the customer, device and order lookups.

Behind an MCP boundary

A separate service with a declared contract. It costs a network hop and a deployment, and buys isolation: their schema changes do not force a redeploy of your agent, and authentication, rate limiting and retries all live in one place.

Right for systems you do not control — here, the ticketing integration.

Have the counter-argument ready

For a single integration, MCP is more machinery than the problem needs, and a plain function tool is the better call. It starts paying for itself when there are several external systems, when a different team owns them, or when the same tools need to be reachable from more than one agent. If Mike pushes on this, agreeing with the pushback and then naming the threshold is a far stronger answer than defending the drawing.

State and context

Two questions come up every time. Does it remember me? — which means loading customer history into the prompt at call start, keyed on caller ID or an authenticated session. And what happens to context on a twenty-minute call? — where the transcript grows, cost grows with it, and quality degrades past a point, so long calls need summarization or a windowing strategy. Neither is exotic, and both are worth raising before you are asked.

Say it out loud ~35 sec

"Your side of this is genuinely small — it's a Python process that registers with LiveKit and waits. When a call comes in, it gets dispatched a job, spawns an isolated subprocess, and that subprocess joins the room as a participant. It makes an outbound connection, so there's nothing you need to expose.

One thing I'd flag on this diagram: a t3.small is a demo box. A Python worker holds something like five to twenty sessions per core depending on your pipeline, so it's tens of calls per machine, not hundreds — and here it's also running the web app and the MCP service. For production I'd separate those and size the workers against your actual concurrency."

Drill

Where does our code run, and can it run in our VPC?

The agent worker is a container you run wherever you like — your VPC, your Kubernetes cluster, your own hardware. It makes an outbound connection to LiveKit, so there is nothing inbound to expose. If you would rather not operate it, LiveKit Cloud will run the worker for you, and self-hosting the media server is also an option if the requirement is that media never leaves your infrastructure at all.

The model hallucinates a tool call with a bad order ID. What stops that reaching production?

Validate inside the tool rather than in the prompt: the function checks the ID exists and returns a structured "not found" the model can speak, instead of throwing. For anything with consequences — a refund, a cancellation — the pattern is propose then confirm, where the model proposes and a human or an explicit customer confirmation commits. And idempotency keys on writes, so a retry does not file the ticket twice.

How many concurrent calls on one box?

On the order of five to twenty sessions per CPU core for a Python worker, and it swings a lot with which plugins you are running — local VAD and turn detection cost CPU, fully hosted inference costs less. So tens per machine rather than hundreds, and I would want to load-test their actual pipeline rather than quote a rule of thumb. The t3.small on this sheet is a demo size, not a production one.

06

SIP from zero

Start here if telephony is unfamiliar. It is a smaller subject than it looks — the vocabulary is intimidating and the underlying concepts are not.

The one distinction everything else hangs from

A phone call is two separate things travelling separately. SIP is the signaling — the who, where and when. Setting up, ringing, answering, hanging up. It is text-based and deliberately similar to HTTP, with requests that have methods and responses that have numeric codes. It carries no audio whatsoever. RTP is the media — the actual voice, in packets, over UDP. Once the call connects, RTP flows between the endpoints, often not even through the machine that handled the signaling.

If you remember one sentence from this section: SIP is the phone ringing, and RTP is the voice. Nearly every telephony question you will ever be asked decomposes into "is this a signaling problem or a media problem?"

The vocabulary, briefly

TermWhat it actually is
PSTNThe public switched telephone network — the global phone system. When you call a phone number you are reaching into it.
SIP trunkThe pipe between your system and a telephony provider who has PSTN access. It replaces physical phone lines with an internet connection.
ChannelOne lane in that pipe, meaning one simultaneous call. "How many channels" is the concurrency question, and it is usually where the cost sits.
DIDDirect Inward Dialing — a phone number that routes to you specifically.
INVITEThe SIP request that starts a call. Think of it as POST /call.
SDPThe payload inside INVITE and its answer: here are the codecs I speak and the address to send audio to. Codec negotiation happens here.
DTMFKeypad tones, carried as signaling events rather than as audio, per RFC 2833/4733 — which is why "press 1" still works over a compressed connection.
REFERThe SIP request meaning "go talk to someone else instead." It is the mechanism behind a cold transfer.

What LiveKit adds on top

An inbound trunk receives calls and can be restricted to specific IP addresses or numbers. An outbound trunk handles calls you place, through CreateSIPParticipant, and the same provider trunk can serve both directions. A dispatch rule attaches to a trunk and decides which room an inbound call lands in — this is your routing logic, so branching by dialed number to send warranty calls and billing calls to differently configured agents happens here. The SIP participant is the caller materialized in the room, created automatically inbound and explicitly outbound. LiveKit will also sell you US local and toll-free numbers directly, or you can bring your own provider. DTMF handling, Krisp noise cancellation and SRTP for encrypted media all come along with it.

Say it out loud ~35 sec

"SIP is signaling only — it's the phone ringing, not the voice. The audio is RTP and it's a completely separate flow. That distinction clears up most confusion in this area.

So a call arrives: the provider sends an INVITE to our inbound trunk, we answer, RTP starts flowing, and a dispatch rule on that trunk decides which room the call lands in. At that point the caller is just a participant — same as somebody sitting in a browser.

And that's really the whole trick. After the bridge, there's one code path, not two. Whatever we build for the web works on the phone, and vice versa."

Drill

We already have Twilio. Do we have to move?

No — you keep your numbers and your provider and point a trunk at LiveKit. Bringing your own trunk is the normal path, and LiveKit selling numbers is a convenience rather than a requirement. It is worth checking whether you are on Flex, though: if the call already lives inside a Twilio contact center, ConversationRelay may fit your existing routing better, and I would rather tell you that than sell you a migration you do not need.

Audio quality over the phone is worse than the web. Why?

Codec. The PSTN commonly gives you G.711 at eight kilohertz narrowband, while the browser is sending Opus at forty-eight. You are losing most of the frequency range before the audio ever reaches speech recognition, so accuracy drops as well. If the customer's provider supports a wideband codec you can negotiate it in SDP; otherwise it is a real constraint worth setting expectations about, and a good reason to evaluate the phone path separately from the web path.

What is a dispatch rule, in one sentence?

A rule attached to an inbound trunk that decides which room an incoming call joins — the routing layer between "a phone rang" and "a participant exists." It is where you would branch by dialed number to route warranty calls and billing calls to differently configured agents.

Does the audio go through your servers?

For a phone call, yes — the SIP bridge terminates RTP and forwards it into the room, and that is unavoidable if you want the phone network and WebRTC to talk to each other. Media is encrypted in transit and SRTP is supported on the SIP leg. If the requirement is that media never leaves your infrastructure at all, that is the self-hosted conversation, and it is a real option.

07

Human handoff

This is the part of the diagram customers care most about, because it decides whether the AI is an improvement on their IVR or simply a new way to trap people.

The failure being replaced is universally understood. You explain your problem to a menu, then to a bot, then to a person who asks you to explain it again. The entire problem is context loss, and every design decision in this section follows from refusing to lose it.

Three patterns, and they are genuinely different

The mechanics of a warm transfer

This is worth being able to narrate, because it is concrete and it shows you have read past the marketing. The caller is placed on hold with audio disabled and hold music playing. A separate private consultation room is created, and the specialist is dialed into it through an outbound SIP call. A transfer agent holding the conversation context summarizes the call to them. Then MoveParticipant moves the specialist out of the consultation room and into the caller's room, the agent sessions end, and the caller and specialist are left talking. The claim worth quoting is that the human can be helping within roughly ten to fifteen seconds, without the caller repeating anything.

When to escalate

Do not say "when it can't handle it" — that is the answer everybody gives. The signals worth naming are risk, meaning money, health or legal exposure; low model confidence about what the customer wants; complexity beyond the agent's scope; regulatory requirement; detected frustration; and explicit request, which should always work immediately, because refusing it is the single fastest way to make people hate a voice agent.

Mechanically, escalation is a tool the model can call, and the tool's description encodes the criteria. That is a nice thing to point at on the whiteboard, because it means the escalation policy is tunable text rather than logic buried in code.

Say it out loud ~40 sec

"The thing everyone hates about being transferred is having to start over. So the design goal here isn't 'can it transfer' — it's 'does the human arrive already knowing the story.'

Because the specialist is just another participant, there are three shapes available. Cold transfer sends the caller somewhere else and the AI disappears — that's the IVR experience and I wouldn't recommend it. Warm transfer puts the caller on hold, briefs the human privately, then moves them into the call, so they arrive informed in about ten or fifteen seconds. Conferenced keeps everyone in the same room with the AI present but silent, which means it keeps transcribing and can file the ticket afterwards.

That third one is what this diagram draws, and it's a decision worth making deliberately — because a silent AI listening to a human conversation is a consent question in some industries."

Drill

Our specialists live in Salesforce Service Cloud. How does this reach them?

Two routes, and I would want to know which fits their operation. Either the agent dials the specialist as an outbound SIP call into their existing telephony, which keeps their current queueing and reporting intact, or specialists take escalations in a browser console that joins the room directly — a better experience, but it is a second place for them to work unless it is embedded in Salesforce. The context package goes across either way; the real question is whose queue owns the routing.

Why keep the agent in the room after the human joins?

It keeps transcribing, so the record stays complete through the human portion of the call. It can be re-addressed for lookups without the specialist leaving. And it can do the wrap-up work — filing the ticket, sending the summary — that the human would otherwise do afterwards. The trade-off is that it is a silent third party on the call, which is a consent and recording question worth raising before they ask.

No specialist is available. What happens?

That is a design decision the customer should make explicitly, and it is a good question to put back to them: hold with the agent keeping the customer company, offer a callback, take a message and file the ticket, or fall back to their existing queue. The failure mode to avoid is escalating into silence — the agent should never hand off to nobody. I would also want a timeout that escalates to a backup rather than waiting indefinitely.

Reliability, scale and cost

The recruiter named reliability explicitly, which means there will be a "what breaks" portion of the conversation. Have this table in your head.

Deployment and rollout

Three properties matter more here than they would for a web service. Draining on deploy means a worker being replaced blocks new sessions and lets active calls finish — critical, because a rolling restart that kills live calls is unacceptable in a contact center. Instant rollback lets you revert to a previous agent version with one command, and in this world a bad prompt change is a production incident, so the rollback story genuinely matters. Cold starts are handled on LiveKit Cloud paid plans by keeping agents warm; self-hosted, that is on you, and it is exactly the problem that makes serverless awkward for this workload.

Observability

Per turn, instrument end-of-turn latency, STT final latency, time to first token, time to first audio, and total response time. Per call, capture duration, turn count, interruption count, tool calls and their outcomes, whether it escalated and what triggered it, and whether it resolved. Plus transcripts, which are simultaneously your debugging tool, your compliance record and your eval set — and a PII problem to be deliberate about.

The mature answer to "how do you know it's working" is an eval set built from real calls, replayed against every change and scored on task completion rather than on vibes. Voice agents regress silently when a prompt or a model changes, and any customer who has been burned by that will ask.

Cost, roughly

Order-of-magnitude figures, so you are not guessing at a whiteboard. LiveKit Cloud tiers run Build (free), Ship at fifty dollars a month, Scale at five hundred, plus Enterprise. Agent session minutes are the main driver at about a cent a minute, SIP minutes run around a third of a cent, and plain WebRTC connection minutes are far cheaper than either. One thing to flag early with a regulated customer: HIPAA and SOC 2 Type II sit at the Scale tier, so there is a five-hundred-dollar-a-month floor before any usage.

Model inference is usually the larger line item and it is not LiveKit's. A useful reflex at a whiteboard is to build the per-minute number in front of the customer — platform, plus STT, plus LLM, plus TTS, plus telephony — rather than quoting a platform price that understates what they will actually pay. It builds trust, and it is the conversation they actually need to have.

Say it out loud ~40 sec

"Let me walk the failure modes, because that's usually where these designs get interesting.

If a speech vendor degrades, I fail over to a second one — and that's the main structural reason I'd keep the pipeline as three components rather than one speech-to-speech model. If a worker crashes, it's process-isolated so it only affects that one call, and it's replaced in about fifteen seconds; I'd be honest that the call in progress is degraded. If your ticketing system is down, I queue the write rather than blocking the conversation, and that's a big part of why that integration sits behind its own boundary.

The one I'd want to design around properly with you is traffic spikes, because agents are heavy — you get tens of concurrent calls per machine, not hundreds, so there's less headroom to absorb a surge than you'd have on a web tier."

Drill

Will this handle 500 concurrent calls?

The media layer will, comfortably — that is what it is built for. The constraint is the agent workers: roughly five to twenty sessions per core depending on the pipeline, so five hundred concurrent is on the order of tens of cores, and I would size it against their actual plugin stack rather than a rule of thumb. The questions I would need answered first are whether five hundred is the peak or the average, how spiky it is, and what acceptable behavior looks like when they exceed it — queue, degrade, or overflow to humans.

What's your uptime story?

LiveKit Cloud targets 99.99% with redundant infrastructure and automatic session migration on instance failure. But end-to-end availability is the product of every dependency — the platform, the speech vendor, the model, the synthesis, the telephony provider — so the honest framing is that the composite number is lower than any single component's. The design work is deciding what degrades gracefully, which is what the per-component fallbacks are for.

How do you know the agent isn't getting worse?

Recorded evals. Take a corpus of real calls, replay them against every prompt or model change, and score task completion, escalation rate and turn latency. Voice quality regressions are invisible in unit tests and obvious in aggregate metrics. Anyone who has shipped one of these has been burned by a model update quietly changing behavior, so I would want this in place before go-live rather than after.

Whiteboard hygiene

Speaking practice

You are being assessed on how you sound explaining this, not on whether you know it. Those are different skills, and only one of them improves by reading. Everything in this section is meant to be said out loud, standing up, ideally to a wall.

Warm-up: six sentences from memory

Before each practice session, say these six aloud without looking. They are the load-bearing claims, and most other answers can be reconstructed from them.

  1. TCP trades latency for completeness, and live voice needs the opposite trade.
  2. The AI is a participant in the room, not a backend service the audio is piped to.
  3. Under one second, because that is roughly where human conversational timing sits.
  4. Co-location beats model tuning, because you pay the cross-region hop four or five times per turn.
  5. SIP is the ringing and RTP is the voice; after the bridge there is one code path.
  6. Handoff is not a transfer — it is a person arriving in a conversation already in progress.

The ninety-second overview

Rehearse this until it is fluent. It is what you say if Mike opens with "so how would you approach this?", and it is close to what you say again at minute fifty-five.

The overview ~90 sec

"So at the highest level there are two ways a customer reaches you — someone on the website, and someone dialing a phone number — and the first thing I want to do is get those to converge, because after that point everything downstream is identical.

They converge in a room. That's a session, and everything joins it: the customer, the AI, and later a human. The AI isn't a service sitting behind an API — it's a participant, a process you run that joins and subscribes to the customer's audio. That matters because it means putting a human in the call later doesn't need a new subsystem.

Inside the agent, the loop is speech to text, model, text to speech, all streaming, with a budget of under a second from when the customer stops talking to when they hear a reply. Answer these aloud, then check

Give yourself sixty to ninety seconds per answer, out loud, before opening the model answer. Speaking it badly once is worth more than reading a good answer five times.

Round one — the technology

Explain WebRTC to someone non-technical in three sentences.

"It's the technology browsers use to send live audio and video. The important thing about it is that it's built for conversations rather than downloads — when the network hiccups, it would rather drop a tiny piece of audio than pause and fall behind. That's the opposite of how the rest of the web works, and it's why a call stays in sync instead of drifting."

Where does the second go? Walk me through it.

Say the §03 script. The scoring criteria: did you name transport as under fifty, endpointing as the three-hundred-millisecond bulk, first token and first audio — and did you explain the overlap, so the numbers don't appear to contradict the total? If you skipped the overlap, the answer sounds wrong to anyone doing the arithmetic.

What's SIP, in plain language?

"SIP is the signaling for a phone call — it's the ringing, the answering, the hanging up. It doesn't carry any audio at all; the voice travels separately over something called RTP. Almost every confusing telephony question gets simpler once you've split those two apart."

Why is turn detection hard?

Lead with the concrete example rather than the theory — the customer saying "my order number is…" and pausing. Then name the two models and their different jobs, then land on it being a dial with no setting that removes the trade-off, tuned per use case. If you got to "there's no right answer, it depends what your customers are saying," you nailed it.

Round two — the pressure questions

This seems expensive. Justify it.

Do not defend the price. Build the number with them — platform, speech, model, synthesis, telephony — and note that the platform is usually the smaller line. Then move to their side of the ledger: what a deflected contact is worth, what handle time costs them today. If you are regulated, flag the compliance tier floor early rather than letting it surface in procurement.

Our security team says no third-party media. Now what?

Take the objection seriously rather than working around it. It is open source and self-hostable, media server included, so media can stay entirely in their infrastructure — they trade the managed edge for that control. Then ask the diagnostic question: is the requirement about media specifically, or about data residency? Those have quite different answers, and people often say the first when they mean the second.

Why shouldn't we just build this ourselves?

Be genuinely even-handed. The agent logic is not the hard part and they could write it. The hard parts are the global edge, the phone bridge, session migration, and scaling stateful long-lived sessions — all of which are boring to own and expensive to get wrong. If they have a media infrastructure team already, the calculus genuinely changes, and saying so buys credibility for everything else.

What don't you know about this?

Answer it honestly and specifically. Something like: "I'd want to load-test the worker sizing against your actual pipeline rather than trusting a rule of thumb, and I haven't run this at your concurrency. I'd also want to measure the phone leg separately, because narrowband audio hurts recognition accuracy and I wouldn't want to promise you web-quality numbers on a phone call." Naming real unknowns precisely reads as expertise; deflecting reads as the opposite.

Your five years as a Google Cloud customer engineer is the most differentiated thing you bring to this interview. The one that isn't a lookup: where do the workers run?

So the answer is GKE for the workers and Cloud Run for everything request-shaped. That split is more interesting than "put it all on Cloud Run," because it demonstrates you understand what makes this workload different from a web tier.

The reasons a customer might prefer them are data residency, an existing Vertex AI commitment, consolidated billing, or a negotiated enterprise agreement — all commercial, and all valid.

The reason to be careful is that the stack on the reference sheet is there because each component is a latency leader in its category, and swapping for procurement reasons can cost real milliseconds. That is a measurement rather than an opinion, so offer to benchmark both stacks against their actual traffic. Offering to measure instead of asserting is the single most credible thing a pre-sales engineer can say.

Where GCP is worse for this — say it unprompted

Region coverage for low-latency voice is thinner than AWS in some geographies, and if their customers sit somewhere GCP is far from the media edge, co-location — your biggest latency lever — becomes harder to achieve. Naming a weakness in the platform you know best is disproportionately persuasive, because it tells the room your recommendations are engineering judgments rather than preferences.

On checkpoint 8

Buy the phone number. Telephony is the area you flagged as least familiar, and it is the one where a single real phone call replaces an hour of reading. Everything about SIP stops being abstract the moment your own agent answers your own phone.
